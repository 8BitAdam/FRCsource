Alliance Optimization

Picking the three highest-rated robots on your list is almost never the right answer. Alliance optimization is about assembling robots whose strengths compound — not duplicate — and whose weaknesses are covered rather than shared.

Beyond the Pick List Rank

After qualification matches end, every alliance captain has a sorted pick list in front of them — robots ranked by composite score, OPR, or some weighted combination of scouted metrics. It is tempting to treat that list as the answer: pick the highest available robot, then the next highest, then the next. This approach is simple, it is defensible, and it frequently produces suboptimal alliances.

The reason is that a composite score measures individual robot capability in isolation. It tells you how much a robot contributes when playing a standard match. It does not tell you how much that robot contributes when playing alongside two specific other robots — where some of its strengths become redundant, some of its weaknesses are covered, and some of its capabilities interact with the field structure in ways that create or destroy value. Alliance optimization is the discipline of modeling those interactions explicitly rather than assuming the best individual robots will automatically form the best alliance.

This matters most in the middle of the alliance selection bracket — for captains ranked 3rd through 6th, who have access to strong robots but not the singular superstars available to the top seeds. In this range, two robots that are each individually ranked 8th but cover completely different roles can outperform two robots both ranked 4th who are doing the same thing. Understanding how to find and construct those complementary combinations is the difference between a deep playoff run and an early exit.

The Role Framework

Before you can talk about complementary roles, you need a taxonomy of what roles exist. These vary by game, but most FRC seasons produce a recognizable set of strategic archetypes that appear across different robot designs. Naming them clearly lets you reason about alliance composition systematically rather than intuitively.

Primary Scorer
High teleop cycle volume. The engine of alliance point output. Usually the highest OPR robot. Should never be duplicated — two primary scorers of equal strength rarely outperform one primary scorer plus a role specialist.
Endgame Specialist
Reliable high-value climb. In games with 15+ point endgame, a robot that climbs every match is worth more than one that cycles well but never climbs. Consistency here matters more than ceiling.
Auto Anchor
Consistent high-value autonomous routine, ideally one that doesn't conflict with alliance partners' starting positions. Ranking point auto tasks often require coordinated multi-robot auto — this role owns that coordination.
Defender
Disrupts opposing primary scorer. High value when opponents have a robot that would otherwise dominate — the effective cycle reduction from neutralizing that robot often exceeds what a third scorer would contribute.
Flex
Competent at multiple roles without specializing. Often the safest third pick — covers gaps, doesn't create redundancy, adapts to playoff match strategy. Usually lower raw OPR than specialists, but higher net alliance contribution.

No robot is purely one role — a primary scorer that also climbs reliably is more valuable than one that doesn't, even if the climb adds nothing to the composite score that wasn't already captured. The role framework is a tool for categorizing which contribution dominates for each robot, not a rigid classification system.

Complementarity vs Redundancy

The central optimization problem in alliance selection is the difference between complementarity and redundancy. Two robots are complementary if their combined output exceeds what either could produce alone. They are redundant if they are trying to do the same thing, competing for the same field resources, and producing less combined output than the sum of their individual averages.

Where Redundancy Hurts Most

Redundancy is most damaging in resource-constrained environments. If the scoring zone can only process one game piece at a time, two high-volume scorers will spend significant match time queued behind each other. If only one robot can occupy the endgame position, two climbers produce the same endgame points as one. If your robot is already covering a ranking point task in auto, a second robot doing the same task adds nothing — the RP is binary, not additive.

The subtler form of redundancy is positional: two robots that operate in the same physical zone of the field will interfere with each other even if they're nominally doing different things. A robot that sources and a robot that scores both need to pass through the center field area. Two of them doing that simultaneously creates traffic that reduces effective cycle rates for both. Mapping your own robot's movement pattern before the draft — and filtering candidates whose movement patterns collide with it — is a practical step most teams skip.

Where Complementarity Pays Off

Complementarity creates value through specialization and coverage. A primary scorer paired with a dedicated defender creates a dynamic where your scorer operates with significantly less resistance than either robot would face in a balanced alliance. The defender's contribution doesn't show up in their individual OPR — OPR only captures scoring — but it shows up in your scorer's effective cycle rate when opponents can't neutralize them.

Complementarity also appears in ranking point coverage. Many games require specific coordinated behaviors across multiple alliance robots to earn bonus ranking points. A robot that can reliably trigger the endgame RP condition pairs very differently with your alliance than one that cannot, even if their OPR values are identical. The RP robot's value to your alliance exceeds what any individual metric captures.

OPR Cannot See Synergy

OPR models each robot as an independent contributor to alliance score. It cannot capture synergy — the additional value two specific robots create when paired. A defender's OPR is low because defense doesn't generate points. A robot that enables another robot to score more has no way to reflect that in its own OPR. Any analysis that relies solely on individual metrics will systematically underprice complementary robots and overprice redundant ones. Use individual metrics to build the candidate list; use role analysis to make the final decision.

The Snake Draft Constraint

Alliance selection in FRC uses a snake draft — the highest-seeded alliance selects first, then the selection order reverses. The top seed picks first in round one, the bottom seed picks first in round two, and so on. This structure has specific strategic implications that should shape how you build your pick list before selections begin.

R1
First Pick — Fill Your Biggest Gap
Your first pick is made before other teams have drafted. The full field of available robots is still open. This is the moment to address your robot's most significant weakness — the role you cannot cover on your own. If you climb but can't score volume, pick a scorer. If you score but don't climb, pick a climber. Don't pick the highest-ranked robot available if they duplicate your strengths.
R2
Second Pick — Cover the Remaining Gap or Strategic Counter
By second pick, other alliances have made their first selections. Evaluate what you still need and what has been taken. If your first pick covered your primary weakness, the second pick either covers a remaining gap or provides strategic flexibility — a flex robot who can shift roles based on opponent scouting before each playoff match. Also evaluate what your opponents' alliances look like and whether a defender makes sense here.
R3
Third Pick — Backup Robot (Rarely Plays)
The third pick is a backup robot and rarely takes the field unless a primary alliance member has a mechanical failure or rule issue. The priority here shifts from capability to reliability and repairability — a robot with a simple, well-built design that can be quickly understood and fixed is often more valuable in this slot than a high-capability but fragile one. Also consider picking a robot you don't want your opponents to have.

The snake draft also means that your second pick is made under time pressure with imperfect information — the teams selected before your turn shift the available pool and the strategic landscape simultaneously. Prepare a tiered pick list rather than a single ordered list: for each scenario (robot A is taken, robot B is taken, opponent alliance looks like X), know which robot you pivot to. Pre-planning these contingencies is worth more than any real-time optimization you'll do at the table.

Ceiling vs Floor Thinking

Alliance selection often produces two competing frameworks: ceiling thinking and floor thinking. Ceiling thinking asks "what is the best this robot could do for us in a playoff match?" Floor thinking asks "what is the worst this robot could do, and can we survive it?" Both are valid — the tension between them is where most alliance selection debates actually live.

Ceiling thinking favors high-upside robots: the scorer who can put up 14 cycles when everything goes right, the climber who can hit the top bar and unlock maximum endgame points, the auto routine that can trigger a ranking-point-equivalent bonus. These robots win you matches you should lose. They are worth the risk if your alliance is already structurally sound — if your floor is high enough that a bad match from this robot doesn't cost you the match.

Floor thinking favors reliable robots: the scorer who averages 8 cycles but rarely drops below 6, the climber who hits a lower bar every single time rather than attempting a riskier high bar with occasional failures, the robot with a simple and proven autonomous rather than a complex one that breaks under pressure. These robots protect you from losing matches you should win. They matter most when your alliance's other robots have significant variance — a high-ceiling but inconsistent first pick is much safer to have when your third pick is a reliable floor robot who won't add variance of their own.

Check the Consistency Metric, Not Just the Average

Two robots with the same scouted average cycle count can have very different standard deviations across their matches. A robot that scored 6, 8, 7, 8, 6, 7 is fundamentally different from one that scored 2, 4, 14, 8, 10, 4 — same average, very different reliability profiles. For playoff alliance selection, the standard deviation of each robot's match-by-match performance is as important as the mean. A consistent 7-cycle robot is often worth more than a volatile 7-cycle robot, because the volatile one introduces uncertainty into matches you need to control.

Modeling the Opposition

Alliance optimization is not only about assembling your three robots — it is also about predicting how those three robots will perform against specific opponent alliances. In playoffs, you often know in advance who you will face, which means you can tailor your strategy to that specific matchup rather than optimizing for an average opponent.

Identifying the Opponent's Engine

Every opponent alliance has a primary scoring engine — the robot responsible for the majority of their point output. Identifying that robot and modeling what happens to your projected score if that robot's cycle rate is reduced by 40% (a realistic outcome for a focused defense effort) tells you whether a defensive strategy is worth pursuing in this matchup. If neutralizing their engine drops their projected score by 20 points and your defender costs you 5 points in scoring opportunity, the net gain is 15 — a significant edge for deploying defense.

The same analysis in reverse tells you whether your opponent is likely to deploy defense against you. If your alliance has a clear high-value scorer, expect focused defense. Pre-plan how your alliance responds to that scenario: does your scorer have enough agility to evade? Can your flex robot create driving lanes by occupying space near the opponent defender? Does your second pick become the de facto scorer while the primary scorer draws defensive attention?

Ranking Point Math

In a playoff scenario where the series is tied and you need a win, the ranking point structure is irrelevant — only the win matters. In early playoff rounds where a series lead changes the strategic calculus, understanding which ranking point conditions you can reliably achieve and which your opponents can deny is worth explicit analysis. A robot on the opposing alliance that is specifically built to deny your ranking point condition — by occupying a zone you need, blocking a position, or disrupting an auto routine — has strategic value that vastly exceeds what their OPR suggests. Your scouting database should flag this capability explicitly.

Model the Worst-Case Match, Not the Average

Your projected alliance score is an average. Playoffs are decided by individual matches, not averages. Model the worst-case outcome for each robot in your alliance — their lowest individual match performance from the event — and ask whether your alliance can still win if two of the three robots have their worst match simultaneously. If the answer is yes, you have a robust alliance. If the answer is no, identify which robot represents the most variance and consider whether there is a more consistent alternative available.

Putting It Into the Database

Alliance optimization analysis belongs in your database as a derived layer sitting above the normalized team metrics. The inputs are your normalized team ratings, the role classifications you assigned during event analysis, and the known game structure. The outputs are per-candidate alliance contribution scores for each possible first pick given your robot's known capabilities, and a tiered contingency list that covers the top six to eight scenarios you might face during the draft.

The most practical implementation for most teams is a dedicated alliance selection table that is separate from the main scouting dataset. It is built the night before alliance selections open, updated after the final qualification match, and locked once selection begins. It contains: every available team's rank, role, average metrics, consistency score, a complementarity rating against your own robot, and a flag for any strategic notes that the data doesn't capture — known mechanical issues, teams that have expressed interest in being picked by specific alliances, robots that play a role the numbers don't show.

The flag column is as important as any numeric field. A team with a slightly lower composite score who has expressed a strong preference to join your alliance is worth picking over a slightly higher-ranked team who will decline your invitation and force you to move down the list under time pressure. Information that only exists in human conversations needs to flow into the database before selections begin.

The Selection Table Is a Living Document

Your alliance selection table should be updated in real time during the draft — not just before it. As other alliances make picks, mark those robots unavailable and re-run your contingency analysis. Teams ranked 3 and 4 on your backup list may jump to position 1 when robots above them are taken. Designate one person to manage the table during selections while the strategist focuses on the conversation and decision-making. The data operator and the decision-maker should not be the same person.
