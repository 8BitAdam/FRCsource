Risk Analysis

Every strategic decision in FRC is a bet made under uncertainty. Whether you are choosing a pick at alliance selection, deciding how aggressively to play a match, or trusting a cycle count your scouts collected at 8 AM — the question is never whether risk exists. It is whether you have thought clearly enough about it to make the right call anyway.

What Risk Actually Means in FRC

In competition strategy, risk is not a vague feeling of unease about a decision. It is a specific property of a situation: the possibility that the outcome you are counting on does not materialize, and the magnitude of the consequences if it does not. Every strategic call your team makes — on the field, at alliance selection, in pre-match strategy meetings — involves both of those dimensions. The probability something goes wrong, and how badly it hurts if it does.

Teams that do not think about risk explicitly still encounter it constantly. They just respond to it reactively rather than proactively — realizing too late that their alliance partner's high-variance climb attempt was the load-bearing element of a strategy that had no backup, or discovering at alliance selection that a robot they ranked second on their pick list has only three matches of scouting data behind it. Risk does not disappear when you ignore it. It just becomes harder to manage.

This article works through risk analysis at three levels: the data your decisions are based on, the in-match strategic choices your drive team and strategist make, and the alliance selection process where risk and reward interact most visibly. The goal is not to eliminate risk — in a competitive environment, the teams that take zero risks tend to leave significant performance on the table. The goal is to make risk decisions deliberately, with a clear understanding of what you are accepting and why.

Risk in Your Data

Before you can make sound strategic decisions, you have to know how much to trust the data those decisions are based on. Scouting data is not ground truth — it is an estimate produced by human observers under time pressure, aggregated across matches with varying conditions. Every number in your dataset carries an implicit confidence level, and treating all numbers as equally reliable leads to decisions that look rational on paper but are built on shaky foundations.

Sample Size and Confidence

The most obvious dimension of data confidence is sample size: how many matches has a robot been scouted in? A team with twelve scouted matches has a much more stable average than a team with three. The three-match sample is vulnerable to outliers in both directions — a single exceptional match or a single breakdown match can move the average significantly in a way that twelve matches would absorb without much noise. At alliance selection, a robot you have seen in three matches is a much higher-uncertainty pick than a robot you have seen in ten, even if their averages look identical.

A useful mental model is to think about the range of plausible true values behind an average, not just the average itself. If Robot A has averaged 6.0 cycles across ten matches with low variance, their plausible true ability range might be 5–7. If Robot B has averaged 6.0 cycles across three matches, their plausible range might be 3–9. The averages are identical but the uncertainty is not, and that difference is decision-relevant at alliance selection — Robot B might be better than Robot A, or they might be significantly worse, and you simply do not have enough data to know.

Variance as a Risk Signal

Sample size tells you how confident to be in an average. Variance tells you something different: how consistent the robot actually is. A robot that scores between 5 and 7 cycles in every match is a different asset from one that scores 2 cycles in half their matches and 10 in the other half — even if the average is the same. The consistent robot is lower-risk: you can build a match strategy around them and trust that the outcome will fall in a predictable range. The high-variance robot is a gamble: sometimes they give you more than the consistent robot would, but sometimes they give you far less, and you cannot control which version shows up on a given match day.

High variance in scouting data can come from two sources, and they are worth distinguishing. The first is genuine robot variance — the robot actually does perform inconsistently, whether due to mechanism reliability, driver consistency, or strategic adaptability. This is real information about the robot. The second is observer variance — the robot is consistent but different scouts are counting differently, or the form design is producing inconsistent data for a field that should be stable. This is noise in your measurement, not signal about the robot. If variance is high only for fields that tend to have definition ambiguity, that is a standardization problem. If variance is high across all fields for a given robot, that might be the robot.

Recency and Conditions

Data from earlier in a competition day is not equivalent to data from later in the day. Robots frequently perform differently after pit crews make adjustments, after drivers warm up, or after an alliance partner configuration changes. A robot that scored two cycles in their first match and seven in their last three may be in a very different mechanical state now than their event average suggests. When evaluating data for alliance selection, weight recent matches more heavily than the overall average, and look at performance trend — is this robot getting better or regressing?

Field conditions and alliance context also affect what a given match's data tells you. A robot that scored eight cycles while playing with a dominant first pick and against weak defense is operating under different conditions than one that scored six while playing defense themselves and supporting a weaker alliance. The raw number does not capture this context. Your scouts' notes do — or should — and qualitative context is often what separates a good alliance selection read from a naive average comparison.

Confident Numbers Are Not Accurate Numbers

One of the most dangerous properties of a well-formatted dataset is that precise-looking numbers feel credible regardless of whether they are. "6.4 cycles per match" looks authoritative whether it is based on fifteen matches or two. When presenting data to a strategy lead or drive team, always communicate the confidence level behind a number alongside the number itself. A robot's average means something very different depending on whether it is backed by twelve matches of consistent data or three matches from which one is already known to be anomalous.

In-Match Risk Decisions

During a match, risk decisions happen in real time under significant pressure. The drive team cannot pause the match to run an analysis. The strategist calling plays does not have a spreadsheet open. The decisions that matter most happen in seconds, and whether they are good decisions depends almost entirely on whether the risk framing was done in advance — in pre-match strategy and in the drive team's training — rather than in the moment.

High-Risk, High-Reward Plays

Many FRC games include scoring actions with asymmetric payoffs: attempting a high climb that is worth significantly more than a low climb, attempting a difficult autonomous routine that scores more but fails more often, or going for a bonus threshold that requires all three alliance robots to contribute precisely. These are high-risk, high-reward plays. They are not inherently good or bad — whether they are worth attempting depends on the match context.

The key questions are: what is the expected value of the high-risk play versus the safe alternative, and what is the match state that makes each one appropriate? A team that is comfortably ahead with two minutes left should usually take the safer path — the additional upside of a risky play does not improve a winning outcome, but the downside of a failure could convert a comfortable win into a contest. A team that is behind and needs to close a gap has the opposite calculus: the safe play preserves the margin by which they lose, while the high-risk play is the only path to victory. Risk tolerance should scale with match state, not stay fixed across all situations.

Autonomous and Early Match Risk

Auto routines present a specific version of the risk problem: teams typically have a "safe" auto and a "high-value" auto, and the choice between them is made in the final seconds before the match starts. The trap is always running the aggressive auto because it looks better on average without accounting for the penalty of a failure. A failed aggressive auto can leave the robot in a bad field position, waste the scoring opportunity entirely, or interfere with an alliance partner — all of which have downstream effects on teleop that the raw auto point comparison does not capture.

Pre-match auto decisions should be based on a clear assessment of three things: the current robot's known reliability rate for the aggressive routine, the field state (is a partner robot likely to be in the zone where a failed auto would leave you?), and the opponent alliance's likely auto strength. Running the aggressive auto against a dominant opposing auto when you have had reliability issues is a different decision from running it when your opponents are weak and a few extra auto points could decide the match.

Defense and Counter-Strategy

Deploying a defensive robot is itself a risk decision. A robot playing defense is not scoring — you are trading guaranteed scoring contribution for the possibility of disrupting an opponent's contribution, which is a probabilistic outcome that depends heavily on how effective your defense is and how vulnerable the opponent is to it. If your defense robot is capable but your opponents are not particularly cycle-dependent, defense is a losing trade. If your opponents have a single dominant scorer and your defense can reliably reduce their output, the math changes.

The risk in counter-strategy is assuming that disruption will work as planned. An opponent's driver who is good at avoiding defense will absorb your defensive resource for the whole match without significant disruption. Assess defense opportunities against what you know about the specific driver, not just the robot's cycle average on paper.

Pre-Match Risk Checklists

The drive team cannot run a risk analysis mid-match, but they can execute a pre-match decision framework that was built with risk in mind. Before each match, your strategist should answer three questions: what is the one thing that would most likely flip this match against us, what can we control to reduce that probability, and what is our plan if it happens anyway? This is not a full risk model — it is a targeting exercise that forces explicit acknowledgment of the highest-probability failure mode rather than assuming the planned strategy will simply execute cleanly.

Risk at Alliance Selection

Alliance selection is the highest-stakes risk decision most scouting programs will ever directly influence. The picks you make determine your playoff alliance. A good pick amplifies your capabilities; a high-variance pick that underperforms can end a strong qualification run in the quarterfinals. Understanding risk explicitly — not just ranking robots by their average — is what separates alliance selection analysis that performs well from analysis that looks good in a spreadsheet.

Upside vs Floor

When comparing two robots at a similar average performance level, the right question is not which one looks better on average — it is what their floor is in a poor match and what their ceiling is in a great one. A robot with a floor of 4 cycles and a ceiling of 8 is a different risk profile from one with a floor of 1 and a ceiling of 11. The average might be similar, but your playoff result depends on which version shows up, and you cannot control that.

For a first pick, floor matters enormously. You are building your entire playoff strategy around this robot's contribution. A high-ceiling but low-floor robot as your first pick means your playoff runs are either dominant or collapse — and you have no control over which. For a second or third pick, the calculus can shift: if you already have a reliable first pick, you might accept more variance in a second pick in exchange for a higher ceiling, because the first pick provides a stable floor for the alliance even if the second pick has an off match.

Capability Gaps and Redundancy

Alliance selection risk is not only about performance level — it is also about capability coverage. If your alliance needs all three robots to contribute to a specific bonus threshold, and one of them has a reliability issue with that mechanism, your entire bonus scoring path has a single point of failure. Analyzing risk at alliance selection means mapping what your alliance needs to win against the reliability profiles of the robots you are considering — not just which robot averages the most points.

Redundancy is undervalued. An alliance where two robots can cover a critical scoring function is substantially more resilient than one where a single robot must perform it. A second pick that provides redundancy on a function your first pick sometimes fails is often more valuable than a nominally higher-performing second pick that leaves you fully exposed when the first pick has a bad match.

Robot Reliability and Mechanical Risk

Playoff matches happen quickly. A robot that brownouts in qualification matches will brownout in playoffs. A mechanism that jams under defense in qualifications will jam under defense in playoffs — probably more, because playoff defense is more aggressive. Scouting notes on reliability are not soft data that can be discounted in favor of the clean number in the cycle count column. They are risk information that the cycle count column cannot represent.

Before finalizing any alliance selection pick, review the notes column for that robot's full event. Consistent notes about mechanism issues, brownouts, defense vulnerability, or inconsistent autonomous performance are patterns that predict playoff behavior. A robot who has logged two brownout flags across their matches is a meaningfully different risk proposition than one with zero, regardless of what their point average says.

The Conversation Your Data Cannot Have

Alliance selection strategy meetings between teams happen during the selection process itself, and the things you learn in those conversations can shift a risk assessment dramatically. A team that honestly tells you their climber has been unreliable all event is giving you information your scouting data may not clearly capture. A team that talks through how their robot interfaces with your scoring strategy can confirm or deny assumptions your analysis was built on. Scouting data sets the prior; pit scouting and alliance conversations update it. Both inputs belong in the final risk assessment.

Knowing Your Risk Tolerance

Risk analysis is not value-neutral. Two teams looking at the same data will make different picks if they have different risk tolerances, and both decisions can be correct given different strategic contexts. A team that is the first seed with a dominant robot may rationally choose the most reliable second pick available because they do not need upside — they need to not lose. A third seed with a weaker robot may rationally accept higher variance in a pick because they need the ceiling to compete with stronger alliances.

Your seeding position, your own robot's reliability, the composition of other alliances, and how many matches of data you have behind each candidate all feed into a risk tolerance calculation that is specific to your situation at that event. There is no universal answer to "should we take the safe pick or the high-upside pick?" There is only the right answer for your alliance at this event, given what you know.

What you can do in advance is build the habit of explicitly asking the risk tolerance question before every significant decision — at alliance selection, before a high-stakes auto choice, when designing a playoff strategy. The teams that do this consistently are not more risk-averse than teams that do not. They are more deliberate. And deliberate risk decisions, even ones that do not work out, leave you with something to learn from. Unexamined risk decisions just leave you wondering what went wrong.

Carrying Risk Thinking Into Competition

The practical value of risk analysis is not that it produces a single correct answer to every strategic question. It is that it changes the quality of the questions you ask. "Which robot should we pick second?" is a worse question than "Which robot gives us the best floor for the playoff scenarios we are most likely to face, given the reliability concerns in our scouting notes?" The second question forces engagement with uncertainty. The first can be answered by looking at a sorted column in a spreadsheet.

Carry three habits into competition. Before trusting any data point, ask how many observations it is based on and how consistent those observations were. Before committing to a strategy, ask what happens if the one thing you are counting on does not materialize. Before making a significant pick or strategic call, ask whether you are accepting risk you have consciously weighed or risk you have simply not noticed yet.

The teams that do this well do not avoid bad outcomes entirely — no one does. But they arrive at those outcomes having made the best available decision with the information they had. That is the actual goal. In a competition environment where luck, mechanical failure, and opponent quality are all genuinely outside your control, the quality of your decision process is the one thing that reliably stays in your hands.
