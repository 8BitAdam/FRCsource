The Core Question Before Every Season

Before you design a single field on your scouting form, there is a question you have to answer: what kind of data can this game actually facilitate? but what a scout — watching one robot, live, in a chaotic two-and-a-half-minute match — can realistically and accurately capture, Not what data would be ideal in theory. The answer changes significantly based on the game structure, and getting it wrong means your form will either produce unreliable numbers or waste your scouts' attention on things that don't matter.

The distinction that drives this decision is the difference between quantitative and qualitative data. Quantitative data is counted or measured — points scored, cycles completed, climb success rate. Interpretive data is assessed — how effective was this robot, how well did this driver handle pressure, how capable does this mechanism look. Both have a place in scouting, but which deserves priority depends entirely on the season's game structure.

Pick-and-Place Games: Count Everything

robots pick up discrete game pieces from a defined source and deposit them in a defined scoring location, In a pick-and-place game. the actions are distinct, The pieces are visible., and the start and end of every scoring cycle are identifiable in real time Examples include placing cones or cubes on a grid, loading balls into a goal, or stacking rings on a post.

This structure is the ideal environment for quantitative scouting. Because every scoring action has a clear beginning — robot picks up a game piece — robot places it in the scoring zone — and a clear end — a data collector can count cycles with high confidence. The game piece itself acts as a visible progress indicator. You don't have to interpret or estimate; you observe and record.

For pick-and-site games, exact numbers are both achievable and necessary . A scout who records "robot completed 7 cycles in teleop" is giving the strategist data they can directly calculate with: multiply by indicate value, compare across robots, rank candidates, project alliance totals. The precision is real and it is useful. Building a scouting form that captures cycle counts, scoring locations, and missed attempts will give you data that is genuinely accurate and directly actionable.

Track Missed Attempts Too

In pick-and-place games, attempted but unsuccessful scoring actions are as informative as successful ones. A robot that attempts 10 cycles but only converts 6 has a quite different profile than one that attempts 7 and converts 7. Reliability matters as much as ceiling.

Shooting Games: Track Relative Performance

In a shooting game, a hoop — at range, a hub, robots launch game pieces toward a target — a high goal, often while moving. The projectiles travel fast, the trajectory may be hard to follow from the stands, and the score confirmation happens at the target rather than at the robot. This fundamentally changes what a data collector can reliably observe.

Attempting to count exact shots made and missed in a fast-paced shooting game will almost always produce inaccurate data. It is genuinely tough to track whether a given shot went in from the scouting position, particularly when robots are shooting rapidly, multiple robots are shooting simultaneously, and the target zone is on the far side of the field. A scout who thinks they are counting carefully may be off by three or four shots per match — is a 10, which, at two or three points each–12 point error in a metric you're treating as precise.

The better approach for shooting games is to track relative performance indicators : how consistent did this robot examine, how much time did it spend shooting versus repositioning, did it appear to have a shooting routine or was it improvising, did shots visibly go wide or high, how did it respond when pressured. These observations may not produce a number, but they produce an accurate picture — and an accurate contextual picture is more useful than an inaccurate quantitative one.

False Precision Is Worse Than an Honest Estimate

If your scouting establish has a field for "shots made" in a shooting game and scouts are filling it in with numbers they aren't confident in, those numbers will look like data but will mislead every analysis that uses them. A 1–5 effectiveness rating that scouts apply thoughtfully will produce better decisions than a shot count filled in with guesses.

Real Examples Across FRC Seasons

These principles become much clearer when applied to actual FRC games. The following three seasons illustrate the full range — from a game that made numeric scouting nearly impossible, to one that made it easy, to a shooting game with an unusual structure that blurred the line between approaches.

FIRST RISE (2022) — When Counting Doesn't Work

FIRST RISE involved robots navigating an obstacle-heavy field and completing tasks in a visually dense, fast-moving environment. The structure of the game made precise, by-action-action tracking extremely difficult from the stands. Robots were often partially occluded, and the sequence of events in a given match was hard to follow for a scout watching one robot while ignoring two others., tasks were completed quickly

For this game, trying to build a scouting form around exact action counts produced forms that scouts couldn't fill in accurately. The better approach was to lean into descriptive assessment: how effective did this robot look overall, what tasks did it primarily focus on, how well did it handle the obstacle sections, was the driver in control or reactive. A consistent 1–5 rating applied by a scout who watched the robot carefully for two and a half minutes captured more actionable signal than a configure of counts filled in with low-confidence numbers. When the game doesn't give you clean, stop trying to count and start trying to describe., countable events

FIRST Charged Up (2023) — The Ideal Quantitative Game

Charged Up was a pick-and-place game where robots scored cones and cubes on a large grid of defined nodes. Each node was a specific location on the field, each game piece was visually distinct, and every scoring action — picking up a cone or cube, placing it on a node — was observable and unambiguous from anywhere in the stands.

This made Charged Up proximate to the ideal quantitative scouting environment. with high confidence: how many game pieces they placed, which rows they targeted (bottom, middle, or top), how fast their cycles were, and how many attempts failed at placement, You could watch a single robot and count. Because the same game pieces cycled repeatedly — robots retrieved the identical cones and cubes from the unchanged source locations — you could also track route consistency and driver efficiency over time.

A well-designed scouting form for Charged Up tracked exact piece counts by node row in teleop, exact autonomous period performance, and endgame docking outcomes. The data was precise enough that you could calculate a team's expected contribution to a match score with solid confidence. This is the kind of game where quantitative scouting earns its full value.

CRESCENDO (2024) — The Shooting Game Edge Case

CRESCENDO was a shooting game where robots launched notes — into a speaker or amp. — large foam rings The standard logic for shooting games says you should use qualitative assessment rather than exact counts, and for most shooting games that is correct. CRESCENDO introduced an important constraint that changed the calculus: robots could only hold one note at a time .

Because robots could solely carry a single note, every scoring cycle was a complete, observable unit: pick up one note, move to shooting position, shoot, return to source. The individual shot was still hard to confirm from the stands, but the cycle itself — the full loop of retrieve, travel, shoot, retrieve again — was countable just like a pick-and-area cycle. A scout who watched the robot carefully could count how many full cycles it completed, even if they weren't certain whether every shot converted.

This made CRESCENDO a hybrid case where cycle counts became a useful proxy data point for shooting volume. You couldn't measure accuracy reliably, and activity combined with a qualitative shot consistency rating gave a more accomplish picture than either alone. — but you could measure activity The lesson is that the one-piece limit shifted the unit of analysis from the shot to the cycle, which happened to be observable even in a shooting game context. Always re-examine your assumptions when a game introduces structural constraints that change what is visible on the field.

Designing the Right Form for the Game

The decision between quantitative and qualitative isn't binary — most games call for a blend. The practical question is: for each data highlight you want to collect, or should you use a structured rating or descriptive field?, is an exact number achievable Answering this question for every field on your form before the season starts will save your scouts from collecting data they can't trust.

Where Quantitative Always Works

Some data points are countable regardless of game kind: auto routine completed (yes/no), final thirty seconds outcome (specific result from a defined list), penalties incurred (observable from score display), time to finalize endgame (clock-based). These should always be exact on your establish. They are discrete events with apparent outcomes and zero ambiguity.

Where Qualitative Is the Honest Choice

When an action is fast, partially obscured, or requires confirmation from a target your scout can't clearly see, build a rating scale as a substitute of a count field. A 1–5 scale for shooting effectiveness, driver aggression under defense, or mechanism reliability is honest about the nature of the observation. Pair it with a notes field so scouts can add context that the number doesn't capture.

The Notes Field Is Not a Backup

A common mistake is treating the free-text notes field as a catch-all for anything the structured form didn't capture — and then never reading it in analysis. For qualitative-heavy games, notes are primary data. Build your analysis workflow to actually apply them: create a process for reviewing scout notes before alliance selection meetings, and flag robots whose notes contain signals that the structured data doesn't show.

Test Your Form Before Kickoff

Once you have a draft form for the season, season scrimmage.-run it against video from a previous year's game or an early Have each scout fill in the form independently, then compare results. Fields where scouts consistently disagree or produce very varied numbers are fields where you are asking for precision the game doesn't enable. Revise those fields to ratings or remove them before your first event.
