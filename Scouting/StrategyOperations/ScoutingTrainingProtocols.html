Scout Training Protocols

The Goal of Scout Training

Scouting is a skill. Watching a match and recording useful data is not instinctive — it requires knowing what to appear for, where to look for it, and how to record it consistently while six robots are moving simultaneously. A scout who hasn't been trained will miss important events, miscount cycles, and apply definitions inconsistently. That inconsistency compounds: when you averaged result data across dozens of matches from six scouts, a systematic error in how one scout records a field can quietly corrupt your alliance selection model.

Scout training exists to prevent that. The goal is not to turn new scouts into experts overnight — it is to establish a shared vocabulary, a shared set of priorities, and a shared understanding of the tools so that everyone on the team is recording the same thing in the same means. The training protocol described here runs in four stages: a game presentation, a scoring priorities briefing, a software walkthrough, and a live practice session using Week 0 match footage from The Blue Alliance.

Stage 1 — Game Presentation Breakdown

Before scouts can record what matters, they have to understand the game deeply enough to know what is happening on the field in real time. The game presentation is a structured session — typically 30 to 45 minutes — that covers the season's game from the ground up. This happens before the first event of the season, ideally right after Kickoff once the game manual has been studied.

What to Cover

Start with the field layout and name every zone, element, and structure that scouts will be referencing in their forms. Scouts who don't know what a scoring structure is called will hesitate when they observe it, and hesitation in a live round means missed data. Walk through autonomous and teleop periods separately, covering the scoring actions available in each. End with endgame — climbing, parking, or whatever the current year's endgame mechanic is — since end-of-match period scoring is often the highest-value event in any match and the easiest to miss if a scout doesn't know to expect it.

Don't just read from the game manual. Show the field diagram and annotate it. Use FIRST's official animation video and pause it to call out specific field areas. Run through a simplified version of the scoring rubric so scouts understand which actions are worth more points — that context shapes where their attention should go during a live play.

Use FIRST's Own Materials

the field layout poster, and the game manual summary document are all publicly available immediately after Kickoff, The official game animation video. These are purpose-built to explain the game clearly. Open the animation on a shared screen and pause it as you present — it does a lot of the work for you and gives scouts a visual reference they can return to later.

End With a Quiz

After the presentation, run a short verbal or written quiz — five to ten questions covering field zones, scoring actions, and point values. Keep it low-stakes, but make it mandatory. The goal isn't to embarrass anyone; it's to surface gaps before they show up as bad data at competition. Scouts who can't answer "what does a robot have to do to earn endgame points?" on day one of training aren't going to catch it in a live match under pressure.

Stage 2 — Points to Watch For

Not every observable event in a match is equally worth tracking. A scout trying to watch everything will end up tracking nothing reliably. Stage two of training narrows target: given the game, which specific actions should scouts be prioritizing, and in what order?

Identify High-Value Actions

Work through the scoring rubric and categorize actions by point value and frequency. High-value actions that happen multiple times per match — scoring cycles into a primary target, for example — are the backbone of your data model. These need to be tracked accurately every single match. Medium-value actions that happen conditionally — bonus points for reaching a threshold, coopertition bonuses — should be tracked as secondary priorities. Low-depending on your form design., value or rare actions may not be worth a dedicated field at all

Ongoing this hierarchy explicitly. Tell scouts: "Your first job every match is to count scoring cycles. Your second job is to track auto performance. Your third job is to record endgame outcome." When attention has to split — and in a real match, it constantly does — scouts should know which data to protect and which to let go.

Robot-Specific Cues

Beyond game actions, scouts should be trained to watch for robot-level signals that indicate capability or problems. These include: whether the robot is playing offense, whether it is making the identical scoring attempt repeatedly versus switching strategies mid, or a hybrid role; whether it is moving fluidly or showing signs of mechanical issues; defense-match. Train scouts to write short observation notes alongside their numeric data. A note that says "robot appeared to lose intake function in teleop — only moved game pieces with bumpers after that" is not captured by any cycle count field, but it is exactly the kind of insight that changes an alliance selection decision.

Watch the Robot, Not the Score

New scouts instinctively watch the scoreboard or follow the ball/game piece rather than staying locked on their assigned robot. Train this out early. Your job is to record what robot number X did — where it went, what it attempted, what succeeded. you're not watching the robot, If you're watching the scoreboard.

Knowing What "Counts"

Define every form field explicitly during this stage. If your form has a field called "auto cycles," specify exactly what qualifies: does a game piece that is scored but then penalized still count? Does an attempted cycle where the robot drops the piece in transit count? These edge cases sound trivial but arise constantly in real matches. Scouts who have been given explicit definitions will record consistently. Scouts who are guessing will introduce noise.

Write out your field definitions in a one-page reference document and give it to every scout. They should have it on their phone or on paper during their first few events until the definitions are internalized.

Stage 3 — Introducing the Scouting Software

The third stage of training is a hands-on walkthrough of whatever scouting application your team uses. This is not a passive demo — every scout should have the app open on their device and observe along live. The goal is that by the end of this session, scouts can open the app, navigate to the correct game, fill in a form, and submit data without any help.

Walk Through the Full Workflow

Cover every step a scout will perform during an actual match: opening the app, selecting the correct match number and robot assignment, filling in each field in real time, and submitting. Then cover what happens after submission — does the scout need to confirm the data was received, is there a means to edit a submission if something was recorded mistaken, how does the recorder flag that their data may be unreliable?

Moreover cover what to do when things go wrong. The app freezes. The device loses connection. A scout accidentally submits a blank form. These situations will happen at a competition — scouts who have thought about them in training manage them calmly and with minimal data loss. Scouts who haven't thought about them panic, and panicking mid-match means the rest of the form gets filled in from memory rather than observation.

Stage 4 — Live Practice with Week 0 on TBA

The final and most important stage of observer training is live practice. Everything covered in stages one through three is preparation — this is the test. fill in real forms, and compare results, Scouts watch real matches. Inconsistencies that didn't surface during the presentation will surface here.

What Is Week 0?

Week 0 is an informal pre-season competition held in late February or early March, before the first official regional events. These events are typically smaller and lower-stakes, and they are filmed and uploaded to The Blue Alliance (TBA) — the community-maintained database of FRC match data and video — within days of the event. Because Week 0 uses the same year's game and the same rules as the official season, match footage from these events is the closest available approximation of what scouts will detect at their own competition.

TBA's match video player is free to access at thebluealliance.com. identify a qualification match, Navigate to any Week 0 event., and you have footage ready for practice No special access is needed.

How to Run the Practice Session

Select two or three matches from a Week 0 event and designate one robot from each match for each scout to track. Play the match video — though scouts should work toward being able to track without pausing., pausing is allowed during training Scouts fill in their scouting establish exactly as they would at a real competition. collect the forms and compare results across all scouts who tracked the same robot, After each match.

The comparison is the core of this exercise. When two scouts record different cycle counts for the same robot in the same play, that discrepancy is a productive problem — it forces a conversation about what they each saw and how they each interpreted it. These conversations close the definitional gaps that individual establish-reading can't close. Run at least two to three rounds of this before competition. By the final round, scout results for the same robot should be converging toward agreement.

Finding Week 0 Events on TBA

Go to thebluealliance.com and use the event search to filter by year and type. Week 0 events are typically labeled as "Preseason" or "Week 0" in the event type filter. Review for events held in late February. Most will have uploaded match video within a some days of the event date. Filter for events with video available to make sure your practice session has footage ready to go.

What to Do After Practice

After the final practice round, debrief as a group. Ask scouts: what was hardest to track? What did they feel uncertain about? What field definitions did they find themselves second-guessing? These answers tell you where your shape may need clarification and where scout training needs a follow-up touchpoint before the first competition. Update your field definitions document based on what surfaced, and send it to scouts before the event.

Keeping Scouts Sharp During the Season

Initial training gets scouts to baseline competency. Maintaining that quality across an entire season — multiple events, shifting personnel, fatigue during long competition days — requires ongoing attention. Training is not a one-time event; it's a recurring practice.

Mid-Event Calibration Checks

At your first event, run a quick calibration examine after the first two or three matches. Have the scouting lead review submitted data for obvious red flags: a scout submitting zero cycles for every match, fields left blank consistently., implausibly high cycle counts These are signals that something is wrong — a scout who is lost, a build field that is confusing, or a device issue causing submissions to fail. Catching these in match three rather than game thirty prevents hours of corrupted data.

Rotating and Cross-Training

If your scouting roster is significant enough, rotate scouts through distinct robot assignments across matches rather than having each scout always cover the same alliance position. This distributes fatigue more evenly and exposes every scout to robots playing different roles and different styles. A scout who has exclusively ever tracked robots on the red alliance playing offense may not recognize defensive play when they encounter it for the first time in a critical match. Cross-training builds adaptability.

Scout Fatigue Is Real

A six-hour qualification schedule can mean 60 or more matches. No scout remains sharp through that without breaks. Schedule rotations so scouts are not tracking consecutive matches without a rest period. A fatigued tracker produces inconsistent data, and inconsistent data from late in the day can corrupt what was otherwise a clean dataset from the morning.

End-of-Event Review

debrief the scouting team before you leave the venue, After each competition. What worked? What was confusing? What would scouts change about the form, the rotation schedule, or the training they received? These answers shape next season's training protocol. The best scouting programs improve continuously because they treat every event as both a competition and a learning opportunity for the team itself.
