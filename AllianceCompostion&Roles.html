Alliance Composition and Roles

How FRC alliances are built, what roles each robot fills, and why game theory explains the difference between a good alliance and a great one.

What Is an Alliance?

In FIRST Robotics Competition, two groups of three robots play against each other in every match. There is a Red Alliance and a Blue Alliance. To score more points than the opponents during a match that lasts two minutes plus thirty seconds, those three robots must work together. It is different from sports where people compete alone because this competition gives an advantage to groups that function well together. If an alliance has three robots that perform different tasks and support what the others do, they will usually win against an alliance where three robots have the same abilities but also perform many tasks in a basic way.

Alliance composition is all about deciding which robots to team up with, and the reasons behind that choice.,/em. It feels like a big deal in the game. During playoffs, teams pick their alliances on purpose through that selection process. You know, trying to build the strongest group possible. In qualifications, things get random with the scheduling, so alliances just happen. But scouts still have to watch closely and figure out how those teams are working together, or not. I think that part can be tricky to follow sometimes. That decision affects everything, playoffs or not.

Why It Matters for Scouts

Even in the qualification rounds, knowing how alliances are put together can really help with figuring out what might happen in a match. Like, you can guess the outcomes better, or make sense of why some results were surprising. It also shows which teams work well as a pair, I think. That kind of breakdown is what makes a scout stand out from just being okay at it.

The Four Core Roles

Although role names are used in subtly different ways in different seasons, four archetypes are present in nearly every FRC game. A major aspect of analyzing any alliance is learning to recognize each of the roles from scouting data.

🎯
Scorer / Cycler

The primary point generator. Focuses on moving game pieces from source to target as quickly and reliably as possible. Cycle time and consistency are the key metrics to track.

High Teleop Output
🛡️
Defender

Disrupts the opposing alliance's scorers by legally blocking, slowing, or rerouting them. A good defender can neutralize an opponent's top robot and swing a match entirely.

Disruption Focus
🧗
Climber / Endgame

Specializes in endgame objectives — climbing, docking, balancing. Endgame points are often high-value and can decide close matches. Watch for consistency and timing.

Endgame Specialist
⚙️
Support / Utility

Fills gaps — feeds game pieces, holds a zone, performs secondary tasks. Often undervalued in scouting because their contributions don't show up directly in the score sheet.

Alliance Enabler
Common Mistake

For teams with scorer-focused analysis, they overemphasize those statistics and underemphasize defense. One strong defender on an opponent alliance can reduce their teleop score by 30-40%. If you don't track defender performance on your scouting form, you are not collecting alliance level information.

Alliance Selection

At the conclusion of qualifications the eight top ranked teams fill the spots of the alliance leaders for the playoff matches. The teams are paired by using a snake draft method. In this method the first leader selects a team and then leaders two through eight pick one after another. By the time leader number eight picks their second team, the pickers have gone down to seven and up from there. It may be noted that the first and last alliances are filled prior to teams at the positions in the middle selecting their second partner.

Alliances can reject their invitation - sometimes, the better alternative is to be the captain of an inferior alliance instead of the second pick of a superior one. Knowing this, the scouts can give the drive team the best recommendations for accepting, rejecting, or choosing partners.

What Captains Look For

In alliance selection, it's not just about choosing the next best-ranked robot, it's about choosing a robot with a compatible skillset. If you are a scorer, you want an end-game robot and a defender - you don't want a scorer, since another scorer does the same job but doesn't add coverage. This is where scouting data gives an advantage.

Game Theory and Alliance Building

Game theory is the mathematical study of strategic decision-making — and it turns out FRC alliance selection is a near-perfect real-world case study. Several classic concepts from game theory show up directly in how good teams think about composition.

01
Nash Equilibrium
A situation where no player can improve their outcome by changing strategy alone. In FRC, a balanced alliance (scorer + defender + climber) represents a Nash Equilibrium — neither side gains by abandoning their specialized role, because doing so leaves a gap the opponent can exploit.
02
Dominant Strategy
A strategy that is optimal regardless of what the opponent does. Reliable endgame performance is often a dominant strategy: the points are guaranteed regardless of opponent behavior, and they apply pressure that forces opponents to respond.
03
Prisoner's Dilemma
Both alliances stacking scorers feels individually rational — more scorers means more points. But if both alliances do this, neither has defense, and match outcomes become chaotic and luck-dependent. The "cooperative" equilibrium — both alliances using balanced composition — produces more predictable, strategy-driven competition.
04
Expected Value and Risk
A high-ceiling but inconsistent scorer has lower expected value than a moderate but consistent one in a best-of-three playoff series. Scouts should track variance, not just peak performance. A robot that scores 12 points every match beats one that scores 20 points two matches and zero in a third.

Scouting for Role Fit

Knowing that roles matter is only useful if your scouting data actually captures them. Standard match forms often track only scoring metrics — which is why many alliances end up over-stacked with scorers. To scout for role fit, you need to deliberately track metrics for each archetype.

Metrics to Track by Role

  • Scorer: Cycles per match, average cycle time, scoring zone consistency, auto performance
  • Defender: Time spent defending, opponent cycles disrupted (estimate), fouls incurred, mobility in defense
  • Climber: Endgame success rate, climb level achieved, time to complete climb, consistency across matches
  • Support: Secondary actions completed, alliance-enabling behaviors, fouls avoided, field positioning

The most valuable scouting insight is not "Team X scored 47 points" — it's "Team X is a reliable endgame robot with a consistent 94% climb rate that has never defended and scores slowly." That profile tells an alliance captain exactly where Team X fits and what they need to surround it with.

Build Role Profiles, Not Score Sheets

The most powerful output from your scouting system isn't a ranked list by total points — it's a set of role profiles that let alliance captains ask "do I have all four roles covered?" rather than "who scored the most?"
